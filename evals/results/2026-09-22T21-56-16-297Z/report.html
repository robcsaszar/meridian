## One way to separate the three terms

This is a proposal for you to confirm or correct:

| Term | What it is | Lifetime | Can you revoke it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Device** | One client install: a browser profile or an app install. Identified by an id we create, not by the hardware. | Until the user uninstalls or clears data | No. It's just the label the user sees. |
| **Session** | One sign-in on one device | Login until logout, expiry or revocation | **Yes. This is what "sign out that device" actually kills.** |
| **Refresh token** | The long-lived credential a session holds, recorded on the server | Weeks | Yes, and it dies when the session dies |
| **Access token** | The short-lived credential sent with each request | Minutes | Usually not one at a time. It dies when its expiry time runs out. |

So "revoke a device" really means revoking that device's session. The one real design tension is the **access token**. After you revoke a session, that device keeps working until its current access token expires, unless every request is checked against the session record. That trade-off between how fast the cut-off happens and how much each request costs will be the first real decision.

## Separating the three words

Here's a starting model to react to, not a decision:

| Term | What it is | Lifetime | Can you revoke it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Device** | Where someone is signed in: a phone app, a laptop browser. It's mostly a label users recognise ("Chrome on MacBook"). | Long, but often not reliably identifiable | Not directly. You revoke what's on it. |
| **Session** | One sign-in on one device: created at login, ended at logout or revocation. **This is the thing you actually revoke.** | Days to months | **Yes, this is the unit you revoke** |
| **Refresh token** | The session's credential; it's how the session gets new access tokens | As long as the session | Dies when its session is revoked |
| **Access token** | Short-lived proof sent with each API call | Minutes | Usually not. It runs out on its own, which limits how long a revoked device keeps access. |

So "revoke a device" really means **end the sessions on that device**. The main shape decisions follow from that:
- Is a device just a name shown on a session, or something you store and track separately?
- How quickly must a revoked device lose access? That determines whether short-lived access tokens are enough or you also need a denylist or server-side check.

Those will be the first round of questions once the basics below are settled.

**Untangling the three words.** I've written these into the map as working terms. They aren't settled yet.

- **Device**: what the user recognises and sees in the list, like "Chrome on MacBook" or "iPhone app".
- **Session**: the server's record of one sign-in on one device. It's created at login and ended by logout, revocation or expiry. **Revoking means ending a session.**
- **Token**: the credential that proves a session exists.
  - An **access token** is short-lived and often trusted without checking the server.
  - A **refresh token** is long-lived and swapped for new access tokens.
  - Tokens belong to a session. A session isn't a token.

A lot of the mush usually comes from a system with no session record, only tokens. Then there's nothing to revoke, which is why question 2 below matters most.

**Candidate destination (not signed yet):**
> A signed-in user can see each device they're signed in on and sign out any one of them, which cuts that device's access within a bounded time and leaves the others alone.

**Two questions before I chart the decisions:**

1. **Does that destination hold?** Say what's wrong if it doesn't. I left admin or support revoking someone else's sessions as an open question on the map rather than ruling it in or out. If you already know the answer, say so.
2. **What do you have today, and how much effort is this worth?** On what you have: are you using JWTs, opaque session IDs, refresh tokens, or a provider like Auth0, Cognito or Firebase? Which clients do you have (web, mobile, API)? If the code lives in another repo, point me to it and I'll check it myself. The biggest decision depends on this: how quickly a revoked device must lose access, and what each request pays to enforce that. The options range from instant (look up the session on every request) to "within the access-token lifetime" (only refresh is blocked).

Once you answer, I'll bring the first round of decisions: what a "device" means, what triggers a revocation, how it's enforced, what the device list shows, and what happens on the revoked device. Each will have a recommendation and its main weakness.
