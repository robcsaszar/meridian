## The three terms, separated

```
Account ──1:N──▶ Session ──1:N──▶ Tokens
 (you)           (one sign-in on       refresh token: long-lived, tied to the session
                  one client)          access tokens: short-lived, issued from the refresh token
                    │
                    └─ device info: browser/app, IP, name, last seen
                       (a label for display, not its own entity)
```

- **Session** is what you actually revoke. It's one sign-in on one client, created at login and ended by logout, revocation or expiry.
- **Device** is how the session looks to the user. It's a description stored on the session, not a separate table. You can't reliably tell physical hardware apart: Chrome and Firefox on one laptop are two sessions, and that's correct. So "per-device revocation" really means per-session revocation, shown to the user as devices.
- **Tokens** are what a session gives out. Revoking a session makes its refresh token stop working. Its access tokens keep working until they expire unless each request checks whether the session is still alive. That trade-off (how fast a sign-out takes effect vs. the cost of checking on every request) is the one hard choice in this feature. I've parked it until I know your stack.

## Proposed goal (tell me if you'd agree to it)

> A signed-in user can see every place their account is signed in, each labelled by device, and sign out any one of them so that client loses access within a known time limit, while the others stay signed in.

2. **How much effort is this worth to you?** For example, a small self-serve "sign out other devices" button, or a full sessions page with admin tools. I won't guess.

Once you answer, the next step is listing the open decisions: who can revoke (the user, admins, or both), how fast a sign-out has to take effect, what happens to the current session, how sessions get their device labels, and whether a password change signs everything out. ## The three terms, separated

```
User ──< Device ──< Session ──< Token(s)
          label      what gets    credentials that
         (display)   revoked      prove the session
```

- **Device:** the thing you recognise, like "Chrome on MacBook" or "iPhone app." It's a label for display and grouping, not a security boundary, because a device ID reported by the client can be faked.
- **Session:** one server-side record of one sign-in. It's created when the user logs in and ends at logout, expiry, or revocation. **Revocation acts on this record.**
- **Token:** a credential the client sends to prove it has a session. A short-lived access token and a longer-lived refresh token both point back to one session. A token proves a session; it isn't the session.

With those separated, "revoke a device" means: end the session(s) shown under that device label, and make every token tied to them stop working. The real design questions follow from that. How quickly must an access token that's already been issued stop working? Does one device hold one session, or several? How do we tell a device apart from the others?

## Proposed goal

> A signed-in user can see every device that holds a live session on their account and revoke any one of them, which cuts that device off within a stated time limit and leaves every other device signed in.

Is that what you want? Change anything that's off. For example, should admins also be able to revoke devices for a user?

## Two questions

1. **What does auth look like today?** Stateless JWTs, server-side sessions with cookies, a hosted provider (Auth0, Clerk, Cognito, Supabase…), or nothing yet? This matters most. With stateless JWTs, revocation needs a denylist or very short token lifetimes. With server-side sessions, it's mostly a delete.
2. **How much effort is this worth to you?** For example, a small feature or a larger overhaul of auth.

Once you answer, I'll go through the open decisions a round at a time: how one device relates to its sessions, how fast a revoked device must be cut off, how devices are identified, and what the user sees. Each will come with a recommendation and its main weakness.
